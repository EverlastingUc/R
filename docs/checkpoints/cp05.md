# Checkpoint 05 — AI Research Literacy

## Objective

Analyze three foundational neural-network papers using verified publication
details and separate statements from the papers from our interpretations.

## Papers analyzed

1. Warren S. McCulloch and Walter Pitts, “A logical calculus of the ideas
   immanent in nervous activity” (1943).
2. Frank Rosenblatt, “The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information
   Storage and Organization in the Brain” (1958).
3. David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton, and Ronald J. Williams, “Learning
   representations by back-propagating errors” (1986).

The individual notes in `docs/papers/` record each paper's verified citation,
main idea, relevance to Entropy, limitations, and source-access notes. Claims
about the papers are marked **Established**; project connections or
conclusions beyond the text are marked **Inference**.

## What the papers teach us

- **Established:** McCulloch and Pitts show how idealized neural nets can be
  described with logic, while explicitly distinguishing formal equivalence
  from a biological explanation.
- **Established:** Rosenblatt studies how a perceptron learns associations
  and how generalization depends on structure in the example environment.
- **Established:** Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams describe adjusting weights
  backward from output error so hidden units can form useful representations.
- **Inference:** Together, these papers motivate the progression used in
  Entropy: define a simple neuron, connect units into layers, and train by
  measuring and reducing prediction error. They do not establish that our
  small classifier has broad capabilities.
